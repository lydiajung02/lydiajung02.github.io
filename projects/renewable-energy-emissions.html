U.S. Renewable Energy & Emissions Analysis

A statistical analysis of whether state- and subregion-level renewable energy adoption is associated with lower emissions, using EPA eGRID data — 13 hypothesis tests with multiple-comparison correction, then an OLS regression to estimate the size of the effect.

How Did I Do This?

  1. 1. Data & cleaning Pulled U.S. EPA eGRID data at the state and subregion level: generation resource mix (% from Oil, Gas, Nuclear, Hydro, Biomass, Wind, Solar, Geothermal), nameplate capacity, net generation, and emissions (CO₂, CH₄, NOₓ, SO₂). Merged state- and subregion-level tables and coerced mixed-type columns to numeric before anything else.
  2. 2. Exploratory analysis Scatterplots of renewable share vs. total CO₂ (by state and by subregion) both showed a visible negative trend — not perfectly linear, which is exactly why the next step needed formal testing rather than eyeballing the scatter. Bar charts by region also showed the West skewing high-renewable and the South skewing low-renewable, hinting emissions differences might partly track region rather than renewables alone.
  3. 3. Multiple hypothesis testing Tested 13 hypotheses (e.g. "states with ≥30% renewables have lower CO₂ than states below 30%", tested separately per pollutant: CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, CO₂e, annual NOₓ, ozone-season NOₓ, SO₂). Testing that many hypotheses at once inflates the false-positive rate, so I applied two corrections — Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg — rather than trusting the raw p-values from each individual t-test.
  4. 4. Causal inference via OLS Fit a multiple regression of total CO₂ on Hydro, Biomass, Solar, Geothermal, and Oil share, plus Net Generation and Nameplate Capacity as controls (28 subregions, R² = 0.597). Hydro came back with a coefficient of -1,125 (p = 0.001) and Oil at +913 (p = 0.001) — the only two statistically distinguishable from zero. Biomass, Solar, and Geothermal all had wide confidence intervals crossing zero, meaning this sample couldn't establish their individual effect with confidence, even though the overall renewable-vs-emissions trend was visible in the raw scatter.
  5. 5. Results & policy framing States with ≥30% renewables showed ~22% lower CO₂-equivalent emissions, with hydro specifically standing out as the renewable source with a statistically defensible emissions-reducing effect. Wrote up policy recommendations (raise Renewable Portfolio Standards to 35%+ by 2030, prioritize hydro investment where feasible) while being explicit about the limitations: one-year snapshot data, state/subregion-level aggregation (no facility or time-series granularity), and the real possibility of omitted-variable bias from factors like energy policy or demand we didn't observe.
