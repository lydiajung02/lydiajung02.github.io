U.S. Renewable Energy & Emissions Analysis

Executive Summary

Using Python, I analyzed EPA data on U.S. states and grid regions to see whether more renewable energy means lower emissions. I tested 14 specific claims, then built a regression model. I conclude that:

  1. States that get 30% or more of their power from renewables have about 22% lower CO₂ emissions. The most renewable-heavy states also emit less methane and nitrogen oxides.
  2. Among renewables, only hydropower clearly lowers CO₂, while oil raises it. The results for solar, biomass and geothermal were unclear.

Significance of This Project

Whether renewables really cut emissions affects how states set energy goals and where they invest. Using EPA data, I asked: do states with more renewable energy emit less, which renewable sources matter most, and can we say renewables are the cause?

Methodology

  1. Explore the data Cleaned the EPA data and plotted renewable share against CO₂ emissions to see the overall pattern.
  2. Test 14 claims Compared emissions between groups of states, such as high vs. low renewable share, using t-tests.
  3. Avoid false positives When you run many tests, some look significant by chance. I adjusted the results using two standard methods, and both gave the same answers.
  4. Measure each energy source Used regression to estimate how much each energy source, such as hydro, solar or oil, changes CO₂, while accounting for how much power each region produces.

Skills

Python: Pandas and NumPy to clean the data, Statsmodels for regression

Visualization: Matplotlib and Seaborn for charts

Statistics: t-tests, false-positive adjustments, regression

Results & Business Recommendation

Only 3 of the 14 claims held up after adjusting for false positives. States with 30% or more renewables have lower CO₂ emissions, and the most renewable-heavy states have lower methane and nitrogen oxide emissions. The other 11 were not significant. The regression showed that hydropower lowers CO₂ and oil raises it. Solar, biomass and geothermal pointed the same way as hydro, but the results were too uncertain to conclude anything.

The pattern held up after adjusting, but the regression used only 28 regions, so I read this as a strong link, not proof of cause. I offer the following insights:

  1. States with 30% or more renewables emit less CO₂, which supports setting higher renewable targets.
  2. Hydropower shows the clearest effect. The other renewables are unclear in this data, which doesn't mean they don't work.
  3. I couldn't measure things like state policy, demand and fuel prices, so I can't be sure renewables are the cause.

States should raise their renewable energy requirements to at least 35% by 2030 and support energy storage and grid upgrades. Policymakers should also expand hydropower where it is environmentally feasible, with rural and Indigenous communities included in planning.

Next Steps

  1. Use five years of hourly data to check whether the yearly pattern holds day to day.
  2. Add policy, demand and fuel price data, and look at counties or utilities instead of whole states.
  3. Test how combinations of energy sources affect emissions, and whether the results change if the 30% cutoff changes.
  4. Ask grid operators how often renewable power is cut back or backed up by gas during peak demand.
