U.S. Renewable Energy & Emissions Analysis

A statistical analysis of whether state-level renewable energy adoption is associated with lower emissions trends, using t-tests with multiple-comparison correction and OLS regression to separate a real signal from noise across many states and years.

This page summarizes an academic/independent project referenced on my resume. Full code and the underlying dataset live in my GitHub — this write-up focuses on the reasoning and statistical choices behind it.

How Did I Do This?

  1. 1. Framing the question Rather than just plotting a correlation, I framed this as a hypothesis test: does renewable energy adoption meaningfully associate with emissions trends across states, once other factors are accounted for — or could an apparent relationship be explained by chance given how many states and years are being compared at once?
  2. 2. Data cleaning & feature engineering Assembled a state-year panel of renewable energy share and emissions figures in Python, cleaned inconsistent units/reporting gaps across sources, and engineered features to make state-year observations comparable (e.g. per-capita emissions rather than raw totals).
  3. 3. Hypothesis testing with correction Ran t-tests comparing emissions trends across groups of states, but with many states being compared simultaneously, the chance of a false positive from testing alone rises fast — so I applied a multiple-comparison correction rather than trusting raw p-values, to keep the overall false-positive rate honest.
  4. 4. OLS regression Followed up the group comparisons with an OLS regression of emissions trend on renewable energy adoption (plus relevant controls), to quantify the size of the association rather than just whether it existed.
  5. 5. Policy framing Translated the regression output into insights aimed at a policy audience — being explicit that this is observational, associational evidence, not a randomized causal estimate, when writing up recommendations.
